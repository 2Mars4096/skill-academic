# Academic Proposal Workshop

## Overview

This skill turns messy early-stage research ideas into disciplined proposal development. It favors narrowing over brainstorming sprawl, feasibility over cleverness, and explicit interpretation paths over polished but empty prose.

## Start Here

Run this sequence:

1. `Raw Intake`
2. `Core Skeleton`
3. `Feasibility Gate`
4. `Canonical Path`
5. `Proposal Build`
6. `Stress Test`

Do not jump to polished proposal writing before steps 2 to 4 are concrete.

## Round Discipline

Treat proposal development as a sequence of narrowing rounds, not one long brainstorm.

- `Round 1`: extract the best current question from messy notes and identify the missing pieces.
- `Round 2`: compare 2 to 3 candidate framings and recommend one lead direction.
- `Round 3`: lock the primary path, data plan, and minimal evidence needed.
- `Round 4+`: strengthen identification, construction details, threats, and interpretation.

End every round with:

- `Current best version`
- `Main risks`
- `Next questions or checks`
- `Parking Lot`

## Default Rules

- Treat `interesting` and `executable` as separate tests.
- Push until the unit, outcome, mechanism, comparison, and data path are observable.
- Default to one paper, one main question, and one main empirical design.
- If identification is not credible, downgrade the claim to descriptive or exploratory instead of pretending it is causal.
- If the user offers multiple directions, rank them, pick one lead idea, and park the rest.
- Prefer 3 to 7 sharp follow-up questions over one long questionnaire.
- Do not accept vague verbs such as `affects`, `matters`, or `improves` without a concrete outcome and comparison.
- Do not claim a contribution without naming the benchmark literature or the exact gap.
- Do not mention data unless the source, access path, unit, coverage, and construction burden are at least roughly known.
- Until the design is coherent, use compact bullets or tables instead of polished academic prose.

## Raw Intake

From the user's notes, extract:

- puzzle or motivating fact
- setting and population
- candidate outcome
- candidate mechanism
- source of variation or comparison
- possible data source
- why the question matters
- user constraints: time, skills, access, target field, or target advisor

If two or more of these are missing, use `references/intake-checklist.md` and stay in exploration mode.

## Core Skeleton

Convert the idea into a one-line research skeleton:

`In [setting], [variation or exposure] changes [outcome] for [unit], potentially through [mechanism].`

Then force five concrete fields:

- `Question`
- `Hypothesis`
- `Comparison`
- `Data`
- `Minimal evidence needed`

If the skeleton cannot be stated cleanly, the idea is not yet narrow enough.

## Feasibility Gate

Before drafting a full proposal, verify that the project has at least a plausible answer for each item:

- observable unit of analysis
- measurable outcome
- usable comparison or treatment variation
- named data source with an access path
- variable construction plan
- first-pass empirical design
- realistic interpretation of null and positive findings

If fewer than five of these are concrete, keep refining rather than drafting.

## Canonical Path Selection

Choose one primary path. Use `references/canonical-paths.md` when uncertain.

- `Measurement`: the contribution is a new fact, dataset, index, or descriptive pattern.
- `Reduced-form`: the contribution is a credible effect of a shock, policy, rule, or exposure.
- `Mechanism`: the core contribution is why the effect happens, supported by channel tests.
- `Extension`: the project starts from a known design and contributes a new setting, population, margin, or interpretation.

Do not blend multiple paths in the first draft unless the user already has a solid base design.

## Proposal Build

When the idea clears the feasibility gate, draft the proposal in this order. Use `references/proposal-template.md` for the fixed output shape.

1. working title
2. motivation and puzzle
3. research question
4. main hypothesis and alternative hypotheses
5. contribution relative to the literature
6. setting, sample, and unit of analysis
7. data source and data construction
8. empirical design or evidence plan
9. threats, falsifications, and robustness logic
10. interpretation tree for positive, null, and mixed results
11. next-step execution plan

Be explicit about what is already known, what is assumed, and what still needs to be verified.

## Stress Test

Before closing, check:

- What would the first table or figure look like?
- What result would kill the project?
- What is the most likely reviewer pushback?
- Which assumption is doing the most work?
- If the main design fails, what is the clean fallback path?
- If the result is null, what can still be learned?

## Interaction Style

- Push back early when the idea is too broad.
- Narrow with concrete alternatives, not just criticism.
- When helpful, present 2 to 3 candidate framings and recommend one.
- Keep a visible `Parking Lot` for side ideas so the main design stays focused.
- When the user gives materials, separate facts from speculation before drafting.
- Once the design is coherent, shift into more academic language without losing the causal or empirical logic.

Use when the user's thoughts are scattered or key proposal ingredients are missing.

- `references/canonical-paths.md`
Use when the empirical contribution is still ambiguous or there are too many possible directions.

- `references/proposal-template.md`

## Outputs

Depending on the stage, produce one or more of:

- a narrowed research skeleton
- a ranked list of candidate project framings
- a feasibility memo
- a one-page proposal brief
- a fuller proposal outline
- a `Parking Lot` of side paths and future extensions
